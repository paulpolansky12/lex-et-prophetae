The story in scenes

No date. Genesis starts before there is anyone to keep a calendar, and its first eleven chapters cannot be put on a timeline. What it starts runs on through Exodus to the kings, who are on the united kingdom and divided kingdom pages.

Notes

Genesis is one book in two unequal parts. Chapters 1–11 cover the whole human race from creation to Babel; chapters 12–50 follow one family for four generations. The book marks its own sections with the heading “These are the generations of …” (see the Threads). Jewish and Christian tradition ascribe it to Moses. Most modern scholars hold that it was put together over a long time from older sources; where that view explains something you can see in the text, such as a story told twice, these pages say so in a clause and mark it as scholarship.

How the Church reads chapters 1–11. The Catechism says that Scripture presents the Creator’s work symbolically, as six days of work and a seventh of rest (CCC 337), and that the account of the fall “uses figurative language, but affirms a primeval event,” a deed at the beginning of human history (CCC 390). Pius XII taught that these eleven chapters are not history as the Greek and Roman historians or modern ones write it, but do belong to history in a real sense, telling in simple and figurative language the truths on which salvation rests and the origin of the human race and of the chosen people (Humani Generis 38). Within those limits a good deal is left open, and the Fathers did not always agree among themselves. The deep dives give Church teaching first and the Fathers second, and say where they differ.

Dates. None can be given for chapters 1–11. The patriarchs are usually placed somewhere in the first half of the second millennium BC, but nothing in Genesis can be tied to an outside record, and this page gives no dates for them. The ages in chapters 5 and 11 are those of the Hebrew text, which the Douay and the RSV-2CE both follow. The Greek Old Testament has higher figures at many points, and Luke’s genealogy follows it in adding a second Cainan (Lk 3:36).

Names and numbers. The Douay-Rheims follows the Latin and spells many names differently, such as Noe, Sem, Cham, Agar, Lia or Juda. Those spellings are in each person’s details. It also translates some names that other Bibles leave in Hebrew: Eden is the “paradise of pleasure,” the Nephilim are “giants,” Moriah is the “land of vision,” Machpelah is the “double cave,” Paddan-aram is “Mesopotamia of Syria,” Sheol is “hell,” and Ararat is “Armenia.” Verse numbers follow the RSV-2CE. The Douay differs only at the end of the book: it lacks the RSV-2CE’s 49:32, so that 49:33 is its 49:32, and in chapter 50 it runs one verse behind from 50:23.

Quotations follow the Douay-Rheims, with its name forms left as printed inside quotation marks. Where the Douay, which follows the Latin, parts from the Hebrew in a way that matters, the note says what the Hebrew has. The best-known case is 3:15, where the Douay reads “she shall crush thy head” and the Hebrew has he, or it.

Deep dives are being added to the scenes in batches, four at a time. In them, Church teaching is cited from the Catechism (CCC) first, then councils and popes, then the Fathers; each type is marked as named in the New Testament, drawn from the Fathers and the liturgy, or only a parallel; and anything taken from modern scholarship is said to be so.
